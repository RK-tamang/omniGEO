Ask a model to write about your product and it will produce something plausible. Plausible is the problem. Plausible sentences about your own features, written without checking, are how brands end up with content that reads well and is wrong.

The fix is not a better prompt. It is a different order of operations.

## Evidence first, prose second

Source-first writing means every knowledge claim in a draft traces back to something you own or trust: a spec sheet, a call transcript, a support document, a pricing page, a signed contract. The writing happens after the evidence is collected, not around it.

This has a useful side effect: the draft is naturally specific. Specificity is what makes content citable, because an answer engine needs a claim it can lift and attribute.

## The method, in four passes

### Pass 1 — Collect

Gather the raw material for the piece. For a product comparison, that might be feature docs, changelogs, two sales call transcripts and a pricing sheet. For an explainer, it is your own operating experience plus any primary data you have.

Keep the sources in one place. If a claim has no source, it does not go in the draft.

### Pass 2 — Claim map

Before writing, list the 8–12 claims the piece needs to make. For each, write one line: the claim, and the source that supports it.

A claim map looks like this:

- “Setup takes under ten minutes for a five-person team” — onboarding transcript, 12 Aug.
- “The API returns results within 300ms at p95” — engineering benchmark, Q2.
- “Annual billing saves 20%” — pricing page.

This is the step most teams skip, and it is the step that prevents invention.

### Pass 3 — Draft

Write to the claim map. Every paragraph should do one of three things: state a claim, support a claim, or connect claims. If a paragraph does none of those, cut it.

Use concrete nouns and numbers. Replace “powerful analytics” with “tracks 1,000 prompts across four engines.” Replace “trusted by many teams” with “used by 5,700 teams, across 18 countries.”

### Pass 4 — Check

Read the draft against the sources. Any sentence that cannot be traced gets rewritten or removed. Then check the structure:

- Does the introduction answer the title question in the first 80 words?
- Are headings scannable and descriptive?
- Is there at least one table, list or definition a model can lift cleanly?
- Do internal links point to the pages that support the claims?

## A before-and-after

**Before:** “Our platform leverages cutting-edge AI to deliver unparalleled insights that help businesses stay ahead of the competition.”

**After:** “OmniGEO runs your prompt set across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google AI Overviews every day, then shows the share of answers where your brand appears. Teams use it to find the questions where competitors are recommended instead.”

The second version is longer and less impressive-sounding. It is also the version a model can cite.

## What to stop doing

- **Do not let a model invent specifics.** Numbers, dates, customer names and feature claims must come from a source.
- **Do not publish a comparison without checking the competitor.** Wrong claims about rivals damage credibility and invite corrections.
- **Do not hide the evidence.** Link to the source where you can. It helps readers and gives answer engines corroboration.
- **Do not chase word count.** A tight 900-word piece that answers the question beats a 2,500-word piece that circles it.

## What this looks like in practice

In OmniGEO’s [Content Studio](/features/content-studio), the workflow mirrors the method: upload the source material, approve an outline built from the claim map, then draft with a citation attached to every knowledge claim. Review becomes a check rather than a rewrite.

You can do the same thing with a document and discipline. The tooling helps; the order of operations is what makes it work.
