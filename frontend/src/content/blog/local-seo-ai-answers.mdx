A customer asks an assistant for “a good dentist near Indiranagar that takes insurance.” The answer names two clinics and mentions evening hours. If your profile does not have those facts, you are not in the answer — no matter how good your website is.

Local visibility has always depended on accurate, structured facts. AI answers just made the cost of inaccuracy higher.

## Your profile is the source

Google Business Profile, Apple Business Connect, Bing Places and the major data aggregators are the sources answer engines lean on for local facts. Treat them as a database, not a marketing page.

The fields that move the needle:

- **Name, address, phone** — identical everywhere, character for character.
- **Categories** — primary category is the strongest signal; secondary categories add coverage.
- **Hours** — including holiday exceptions and “hours may differ” flags.
- **Services and products** — with descriptions, not just names.
- **Attributes** — accessibility, payments, parking, insurance accepted.
- **Photos** — recent, geotagged where appropriate, varied.

Any mismatch is a reason for a model to skip you. Consistency is boring and it is the whole job.

## Reviews are retrieval fuel

Reviews are not just social proof. They are a corpus of natural-language facts about your business: “open late,” “great with kids,” “took my insurance.” Answer engines read that language.

A working rhythm:

1. **Ask at the right moment** — after a successful visit, not in a bulk email a week later.
2. **Reply to everything** — within 48 hours, in the location’s voice, referencing specifics.
3. **Never template every reply** — duplicated replies look automated and waste the chance to add facts.
4. **Track sentiment by location** — one branch with a 3.9 average is a different problem from a brand-wide 4.6.

Drafting replies with AI is fine if a human checks them. The failure mode is generic, over-friendly text that says nothing.

## Measure street by street, not city-wide

A city-level average hides the only ranking that matters to a branch. Use a geogrid: a grid of points across each service area, each checked for local-pack position.

What the grid tells you:

- Whether your weak areas are geographic or competitive.
- Whether a new location is cannibalising an existing one.
- Whether a rank change is real or a single-point fluctuation.

Review the grid monthly, and after any profile change. A “we moved to a bigger office” update can reset local relevance for weeks.

## The weekly operating rhythm

| Cadence | Task |
| --- | --- |
| Daily | New reviews across all locations — reply queue |
| Weekly | Profile posts, photo updates, Q&A sweep |
| Monthly | Geogrid review, category and attribute audit |
| Quarterly | NAP consistency crawl, duplicate listing cleanup |

This is unglamorous work. It is also the difference between showing up in the map pack and being the clinic the assistant forgets.

## One more thing: the website still matters

Local pages per location — with the address, hours, services, staff and embedded map — give search engines and answer engines a canonical page to cite. Link each location page from the profile, and keep the facts in sync with the profile. A profile that says 7pm and a page that says 6pm is a fact conflict, and conflicts get dropped.
